    What is academic research?

    Academic research is a systematic and ethical process of enquiry. It involves asking a clearly defined research question, gathering and analysing evidence in a planned way, and drawing conclusions that others can examine and challenge. What makes research academic is not the topic but the approach: it is careful, transparent, grounded in existing knowledge and open to scrutiny.

    Systematic

    It follows a planned and logical process, so that each decision can be explained and justified.

    Evidence based

    Conclusions are drawn from data that have been collected and analysed carefully, rather than from opinion alone.

    Ethical

    It protects the rights, dignity and wellbeing of everyone involved, and is conducted with honesty.

    Transparent

    Methods and limitations are reported clearly so that others can evaluate, and potentially repeat, the work.

    Why does research matter?
    • It informs practice. Professionals use research evidence to decide what works, for whom and in what circumstances.
    • It shapes policy. National and local decisions about funding, curriculum and services draw on research findings.
    • It develops critical thinking. Learning how research is produced helps you judge the quality of claims you read in reports, the media and online.
    • It supports your studies and career. Research skills underpin assignments, dissertations and the kind of reflective, evidence-informed practice valued by employers.
    Example

    A teaching assistant notices that pupils seem more confident readers after paired reading sessions. Everyday observation suggests this, but research asks further questions. What exactly is meant by confidence, and how could it be measured? Has confidence really changed, or are pupils simply more familiar with the books? A systematic study would define its terms, select participants fairly, obtain consent and collect evidence before drawing any conclusions.

    The research process

    Most research projects follow a broadly similar sequence of stages.

    1. Identify a topic and research questionNarrow a broad area of interest into a focused question that can realistically be answered with the time and resources available.
    2. Review the literatureFind, read and critically evaluate existing research. A literature review shows what is already known, identifies gaps and helps you refine your question.
    3. Design the studyChoose your approach, methods, sample and ethical safeguards, then apply for ethical approval. This plan is your research design.
    4. Collect dataGather data using your chosen methods, following your approved plan and recording everything accurately.
    5. Analyse dataOrganise and interpret the data, for example using statistics for numerical data or thematic analysis for interview data.
    6. Draw conclusions and reportAnswer your research question, relate your findings to the literature, acknowledge limitations and make recommendations.
    Remember

    Although it is shown as a sequence, the research process is iterative. Reading the literature often leads you to refine your question, and your analysis may raise new questions for future research.

    Key points
    • Academic research is a systematic, ethical and transparent process of enquiry.
    • It begins with a focused research question informed by existing literature.
    • The process moves from question to design, data collection, analysis and conclusions, but researchers often return to earlier stages.

    2. Types of Research

    Research can be described in several overlapping ways: by the source of its data, the type of data, its purpose, its aim and the logic of reasoning it uses. A single study can be described using several of these labels at once.

    Example

    A questionnaire completed by 200 parents about homework is primary (new data), quantitative (mainly numbers), descriptive (it describes views) and applied (it may inform school homework policy).

    Primary and secondary research

    Primary research

    Primary research collects new data directly from the source for your own study, for example interviewing five special educational needs coordinators (SENCOs) about transition planning.

    Advantages
    • Designed to answer your exact question
    • Current and first-hand
    • You control how data are collected
    Limitations
    • Time consuming
    • Requires ethical approval
    • Often small scale

    Secondary research

    Secondary research uses data or sources already produced by others, for example published Department for Education attendance statistics, Ofsted reports or previous studies.

    Advantages
    • Quick and low cost
    • Access to large datasets
    • Fewer ethical risks to participants
    Limitations
    • May not fit your question exactly
    • You cannot check how data were collected
    • May be out of date
    Qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods

    Quantitative research collects numerical data that can be counted, measured and analysed statistically. Qualitative research collects words, images or observations to explore meanings, experiences and perspectives in depth. Mixed methods research combines both within a single study.

    Comparing quantitative and qualitative research
    FeatureQuantitativeQualitative
    Type of dataNumbers, scores, countsWords, images, behaviours
    Typical questionHow many? How much? Is there a difference?How? Why? What is it like?
    Typical sampleLarger, ideally representativeSmaller, selected for depth
    Common methodsQuestionnaires with closed questions, tests, structured observation, experimentsInterviews, focus groups, unstructured observation, document analysis
    AnalysisDescriptive and inferential statisticsThematic, narrative or content analysis
    Main strengthCan compare groups and identify patterns across many peopleProvides rich, detailed understanding of experiences
    Main limitationMay miss the reasons behind the numbersFindings are harder to generalise
    Example

    A mixed methods study of a new behaviour policy might analyse behaviour log data across a term (quantitative) and then interview six teachers about how the policy works in practice (qualitative).

    Exploratory, descriptive and explanatory research

    These labels describe the purpose of a study.

    Exploratory

    Exploratory research investigates a topic that is not yet well understood. It is flexible and often generates ideas for later studies.

    Example: How are teaching assistants using generative AI tools to support pupils?

    Descriptive

    Descriptive research describes characteristics accurately, answering what, how many or how often.

    Example: What proportion of Year 9 pupils have access to a laptop at home?

    Explanatory

    Explanatory research seeks to explain why something happens, often by examining cause and effect.

    Example: Does the type of feedback pupils receive affect their improvement in writing?

    Basic (pure) and applied research

    Basic (pure) research

    Basic research aims to extend theoretical knowledge without an immediate practical application.

    Example: How does working memory develop between the ages of five and eleven?

    Applied research

    Applied research aims to solve a practical problem or improve practice in a particular setting. Most student projects are applied.

    Example: Does a six-week phonics intervention improve the word reading of Year 1 pupils in one school?

    Inductive and deductive approaches

    These approaches describe the logic connecting theory and data. A deductive approach moves from theory to data, testing an existing idea. An inductive approach moves from data to theory, building new ideas from what is observed.

    Deductive research is often associated with quantitative methods and inductive research with qualitative methods, although this is a tendency rather than a rule.

    Common research designs

    Experimental design

    In an experimental design the researcher deliberately changes one variable and measures its effect on another while keeping other factors constant. Participants are ideally assigned to groups by random allocation. Where this is not possible, for example when comparing two existing classes, the study is a quasi-experiment.

    Case study

    A case study investigates a single person, group, organisation or event in depth within its real-life context, often using several sources of data. Example: how one nursery has introduced a key person approach.

    Survey

    A survey collects information from a sample of people, usually by questionnaire, to describe or compare attitudes, characteristics or behaviours. Example: a survey of trainee teachers' confidence in teaching phonics.

    Action research

    Action research is carried out by practitioners to improve their own practice. It follows repeated cycles:

    1. Plan
    2. >
    3. Act
    4. >
    5. Observe
    6. >
    7. Reflect
    8. >
    9. Plan again

    Ethnography

    Ethnography involves immersion in a group or setting over an extended period to understand its culture from the inside. Example: spending a term in a pupil referral unit to understand how staff and pupils build relationships.

    Comparing research designs

    Summary of common research designs
    DesignPurposeTypical dataAdvantagesLimitations
    ExperimentalTo test cause and effect by changing one variable and measuring the effect on anotherMainly quantitativeStrongest evidence of cause and effect; controls other variables; can be repeatedCan be artificial; ethical limits on what can be changed; hard to control everything in real settings

    SurveyTo describe the characteristics, attitudes or behaviours of a groupMainly quantitative, sometimes qualitativeReaches many people quickly; cost effective; easy to compare responsesLimited depth; low response rates; relies on honest self-report

    Key points
    • Research can be classified by data source (primary or secondary), data type (quantitative, qualitative or mixed), purpose (exploratory, descriptive or explanatory), aim (basic or applied) and logic (deductive or inductive).
    • The research design should be chosen because it fits the research question, not because it is familiar.

    3. Research Ethics

    Research ethics are the principles that protect participants, researchers, institutions and the integrity of knowledge. Any research involving people must be planned with ethics in mind from the start and approved before data collection begins.

    Respect

    Treat participants as autonomous people who make their own informed choices.

    Avoidance of harm

    Minimise any risk of physical, psychological, social or reputational harm.

    Integrity

    Conduct and report research honestly, accurately and transparently.

    Accountability

    Follow the law, professional guidelines and your institution's procedures.

    Informed consent and participant information sheets

    Informed consent means that participants agree to take part voluntarily, with a full understanding of what is involved. Consent should be:

    • Informed: participants understand the purpose, what they will do, any risks and how their data will be used.
    • Voluntary: free from pressure. Take particular care where there is a power relationship, for example if participants are your own pupils, students or colleagues.
    • Recorded: usually through a signed or completed consent form.
    • Ongoing: participants can change their minds at any point.

    A participant information sheet gives people the information they need to decide. It should be written in clear, accessible language and normally includes:

    • the title and purpose of the study, and who is conducting and supervising it
    • what taking part involves and how long it will take
    • any possible risks or benefits
    • how data will be stored, used, anonymised and eventually deleted
    • the right to withdraw and any deadline for withdrawing data
    • contact details for the researcher, supervisor and a complaints route

    For children, researchers normally obtain consent from a parent or carer and the child's own assent, explained in age-appropriate language.

    Anonymity and confidentiality

    These terms are often confused but mean different things.

    Anonymity

    Anonymity means participants cannot be identified. In practice, researchers use a pseudonym and remove details such as school names, job titles or locations that could reveal identity.

    Confidentiality

    Confidentiality means that what participants share will not be disclosed beyond what has been agreed. Participants must be told the limits of confidentiality before they take part.

    Important

    Confidentiality is never absolute. If a participant discloses that they or someone else is at risk of harm, you must follow safeguarding procedures. Explain this in your information sheet.

    In small settings, people may be identified from a combination of details even when names are removed. This is sometimes called deductive disclosure. Describe settings in general terms, for example "a small rural primary school in the north of England".

    Right to withdraw

    Participants have the right to withdraw from a study at any time, without giving a reason and without any penalty or loss of benefit.

    • State clearly how to withdraw and who to contact.
    • Set a realistic deadline for withdrawing data, for example "until data are anonymised on 30 April", because data cannot be removed once anonymised or published.
    • In focus groups, explain that individual contributions to a shared discussion may not be removable.
    • Never pressure people to stay, and never link participation to grades, employment or services.
    Avoidance of harm and working with vulnerable groups

    Researchers must anticipate and minimise any risk of harm, including physical, emotional, psychological, social and reputational harm. Sensitive topics, such as bullying or family circumstances, require particular care. Provide a debriefing and signpost sources of support where appropriate. Researchers should also consider their own safety and wellbeing.

    Vulnerable groups include children and young people, adults who may lack capacity to consent, people in care settings and people in dependent relationships with the researcher. Additional safeguards include:

    • permission from a gatekeeper, such as a headteacher or nursery manager, before approaching participants
    • a Disclosure and Barring Service (DBS) check where required
    • following the setting's safeguarding policy
    • using accessible, age-appropriate materials and shorter sessions
    • ensuring another responsible adult is aware of, and nearby during, data collection
    Data protection: UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018

    In the UK, the use of personal data is governed by the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018. Some information, such as health, ethnicity or religion, is special category data and requires extra protection.

    The UK GDPR sets out seven principles. Personal data must be:

    1. processed lawfully, fairly and transparently
    2. collected for a specified purpose and not used for other purposes (purpose limitation)
    3. adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary (data minimisation)
    4. accurate and kept up to date
    5. kept no longer than necessary (storage limitation)
    6. kept secure (integrity and confidentiality)
    7. handled by an organisation that can show it complies (accountability)

    In practice, this means you should:

    • collect only the personal data you genuinely need
    • store data on your institution's secure, password-protected storage, not on personal devices or unencrypted memory sticks
    • store consent forms separately from research data
    • use pseudonyms or codes instead of names as early as possible
    • delete data securely in line with your institution's retention policy
    • use the research privacy notice provided by your institution
    Honesty, integrity and avoiding plagiarism or data fabrication

    Research integrity means conducting and reporting research honestly. Serious breaches include:

    • Plagiarism: presenting another person's words, work or ideas as your own. Always cite and reference sources using your institution's referencing style.
    • Fabrication: inventing data or results.
    • Falsification: changing, manipulating or selectively omitting data so that results are misleading.

    Good practice also includes reporting unexpected or disappointing findings, acknowledging limitations honestly, declaring any conflict of interest and using generative AI tools only as your institution's policy permits, with appropriate acknowledgement. Breaches are treated as academic misconduct and can have serious consequences.

    The ethical approval process

    Every college and university has a procedure for approving research. The details vary, but it usually follows these steps:

    1. Discuss your plans with your supervisorAgree a feasible design and identify ethical risks early.
    2. Complete an ethics applicationDescribe your aims, methods, participants, recruitment, consent, risks and data management. Attach your information sheet, consent form and research instruments.
    3. ReviewLow-risk projects may be reviewed by your supervisor or a department panel. Higher-risk projects, such as those with vulnerable groups or sensitive topics, go to an ethics committee.
    4. Respond to feedbackMake any changes requested and resubmit if necessary.
    5. Receive approval before contacting participantsNo data may be collected until approval is confirmed in writing.
    6. Seek further approval for changesIf your methods or participants change, check with your supervisor before continuing.
    Important

    Collecting data without ethical approval is a form of academic misconduct, and any data collected may not be used in your assessment.

    The BERA Ethical Guidelines for Educational Research

    The BERA Ethical Guidelines for Educational Research are widely used in UK education. The current fifth edition was published in 2024. They are based on an ethic of respect for the person, knowledge, democratic values, the quality of educational research and academic freedom.

    The guidelines set out researchers' responsibilities to:

    • participants, including consent, transparency, the right to withdraw, privacy and avoiding harm
    • sponsors, clients and stakeholders in the research
    • the community of educational researchers
    • publication and dissemination of findings
    • researchers' own wellbeing and development

    Reference: British Educational Research Association (2024) Ethical Guidelines for Educational Research. 5th edn. London: BERA. Available at: www.bera.ac.uk

    Check with your tutor which guidelines apply to your programme. Other professional codes include those of the British Psychological Society and the British Sociological Association.

    Key points
    • Participants must give informed, voluntary consent and may withdraw at any time.
    • Protect identities through anonymity and confidentiality, within the limits of safeguarding.
    • Handle personal data in line with UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018.
    • Obtain ethical approval before collecting any data, and report your research honestly.

    4. Variables and Hypotheses

    A variable is any characteristic, factor or condition that can change and can be measured, manipulated or controlled. Identifying variables clearly is essential for designing studies that test relationships, particularly in quantitative research.

    Independent, dependent and controlled variables

    Independent variable (IV)

    The independent variable is what the researcher changes, manipulates or compares. It is the presumed cause.

    Dependent variable (DV)

    The dependent variable is what the researcher measures. It is the presumed effect, and it depends on the IV.

    Controlled variables

    A controlled variable is kept the same throughout, so that it cannot explain any change in the DV.

    Worked example 1

    Question: Does using a visual timetable reduce disruptive behaviour in a Reception class?

    • Independent variable: use of a visual timetable (used or not used)
    • Dependent variable: number of disruptive incidents recorded per session
    • Controlled variables: same class, same time of day, same observer, same definition of a disruptive incident

    A useful reminder: the IV is the variable I change; the DV is the Data I collect.

    Extraneous and confounding variables

    An extraneous variable is any variable, other than the IV, that could affect the DV if it is not controlled. Examples include noise levels, time of day, pupils' prior attainment or tiredness.

    A confounding variable is an extraneous variable that changes systematically alongside the IV. Because the two change together, it becomes impossible to know which one caused the effect.

    Example

    A teacher tests a new spelling programme with her morning group and compares results with her afternoon group. If the morning group scores higher, is this because of the programme, or because pupils are more alert in the morning? Time of day is a confounding variable.

    Ways to reduce their influence: random allocation to groups, standardised instructions and procedures, matching groups on key characteristics, and testing all groups under the same conditions.

    Moderating and mediating variables (a brief introduction)

    These variables help explain more complex relationships.

    Moderating variable

    A moderating variable changes the strength or direction of the relationship between the IV and DV.

    Example: the effect of homework on attainment may be stronger for older pupils than for younger pupils. Age moderates the relationship.

    Mediating variable

    A mediating variable explains how or why the IV affects the DV. It sits between them in a chain.

    1. Mentoring (IV)
    2. >
    3. Increased self-belief (mediator)
    4. >
    5. Improved attendance (DV)

    Writing research questions

    A good research question is clear, focused, researchable, feasible within your time and resources, ethical and relevant to your field.

    Improving research questions
    Weak questionProblemImproved question
    Is phonics good?Vague; "good" is undefined; no populationWhat impact does a six-week synthetic phonics intervention have on the word reading scores of Year 1 pupils in one primary school?
    How can behaviour be improved?Too broad to answer in one projectHow do early career teachers in two secondary schools describe their strategies for managing low-level disruption?
    Why do all children hate maths?Assumes the answer; impossible populationWhat attitudes towards mathematics do Year 6 pupils in one school report, and what reasons do they give?

    Hypotheses and null hypotheses

    A hypothesis is a clear, testable prediction about the relationship between variables. Hypotheses are mainly used in quantitative, deductive research. Qualitative studies usually use research questions instead.

    Directional hypothesis

    A directional hypothesis predicts the direction of the effect.

    "Pupils who receive daily retrieval practice will achieve higher end-of-unit test scores than pupils who do not."

    Non-directional hypothesis

    Predicts a difference but not its direction.

    "There will be a difference in end-of-unit test scores between pupils who receive daily retrieval practice and those who do not."

    Null hypothesis (H0)

    The null hypothesis predicts no difference or relationship.

    "There will be no difference in end-of-unit test scores between the two groups; any difference will be due to chance."

    Remember

    Researchers test the null hypothesis. Results allow you to reject or fail to reject it. Research never "proves" a hypothesis; it provides evidence that supports or does not support it.

    Worked examples 2 and 3

    Worked example 2: Reading format

    Question: Do Year 4 pupils who read on a tablet app for 15 minutes a day improve their reading age more than those who read paper books?

    • IV: reading format (tablet app or paper book)
    • DV: change in reading age on a standardised test
    • Controlled: 15 minutes daily reading; eight-week period; same test; same year group
    • Possible extraneous variable: amount of reading support at home
    • Hypothesis: Pupils using the app will show a greater improvement in reading age than pupils reading paper books.
    • Null hypothesis: There will be no difference in reading age improvement between the two groups.

    Worked example 3: Seating arrangements

    Question: Does seating arrangement affect time on task in Year 8 mathematics lessons?

    • IV: seating arrangement (rows or groups)
    • DV: percentage of observed time pupils spend on task
    • Controlled: same teacher, same class, same lesson length, same observation checklist
    • Possible confounding variable: if group seating is only used for practical lessons, lesson type would be confounded with seating
    Key points
    • The IV is changed or compared; the DV is measured; controlled variables are kept constant.
    • Extraneous variables can distort results; confounding variables make cause and effect impossible to establish.
    • A hypothesis is a testable prediction; the null hypothesis predicts no effect.

    5. Sampling and Data Collection

    It is rarely possible to include everyone in a study, so researchers select a sample. How the sample is chosen, and how data are collected from it, strongly affects what conclusions can be drawn.

    Population and sample

    Population

    The population is the whole group you want to draw conclusions about.

    Sampling frame

    A sampling frame is a list of all members of the population, from which the sample is drawn.

    Sample

    The sample is the group of people who actually take part.

    Example

    Population: all 1,200 Year 7 pupils in a multi-academy trust. Sampling frame: the trust's enrolment list. Sample: 120 pupils selected from that list.

    A representative sample reflects the characteristics of the population, which increases generalisability. Sample size depends on your purpose: quantitative studies generally need larger samples, while qualitative studies use smaller samples and may stop recruiting at the point of saturation.

    Probability sampling methods

    In probability sampling, every member of the population has a known chance of selection, usually through random selection. These methods require a sampling frame and support generalisation.

    Simple random

    Simple random sampling: every member has an equal chance, for example using a random number generator with a class list.

    Advantage: minimises selection bias.
    Limitation: needs a complete list; small subgroups may be missed by chance.

    Stratified

    Stratified sampling: divide the population into subgroups (for example year group) and randomly select from each in proportion.

    Advantage: key subgroups are represented.
    Limitation: requires information about every member.

    Systematic

    Systematic sampling: select every nth person from a list after a random start, for example every 10th pupil.

    Advantage: simple to carry out.
    Limitation: biased if the list has a hidden pattern.

    Cluster

    Cluster sampling: randomly select whole groups, such as schools or classes, and include everyone in them.

    Advantage: practical for large, spread-out populations.
    Limitation: clusters may differ, reducing precision.

    Non-probability sampling methods

    In non-probability sampling, participants are not selected at random. These methods are common in small-scale and qualitative research, but findings cannot be generalised with confidence.

    Convenience

    Convenience sampling: whoever is easiest to access, for example your own placement class.

    Advantage: quick and inexpensive.
    Limitation: high risk of sampling bias.

    Purposive

    Purposive sampling: deliberately choose people with relevant knowledge or experience, for example SENCOs.

    Advantage: information-rich participants.
    Limitation: depends on the researcher's judgement.

    Snowball

    Snowball sampling: participants recruit others they know, for example home-educating families.

    Advantage: reaches hard-to-access groups.
    Limitation: participants tend to share similar views and networks.

    Quota

    Quota sampling: recruit a set number from each category, for example 10 primary and 10 secondary teachers, but not at random.

    Advantage: ensures subgroups are included.
    Limitation: selection within each quota may be biased.

    Data collection methods

    Choose methods that fit your research question, your participants and your resources. Always carry out a pilot study of your instruments where possible.

    Questionnaires

    A questionnaire is a set of written questions completed on paper or online. It may include closed questions, such as a Likert scale, and open questions.

    Strengths
    • Reach many people quickly
    • Easy to compare and analyse
    • Anonymous responses may encourage honesty
    Limitations
    • Low response rates
    • No chance to probe answers
    • Poorly worded questions lead to unreliable data

    Example: an online questionnaire for parents about communication with school.

    Interviews

    An interview is a conversation in which the researcher asks questions. Interviews may be structured (fixed questions in a fixed order), semi-structured (planned questions with flexibility to follow up) or unstructured (an open conversation around a topic). Semi-structured interviews are the most common in student projects.

    Strengths
    • Rich, detailed data
    • Can clarify and probe answers
    • Suitable for sensitive topics
    Limitations
    • Time consuming to conduct and transcribe
    • Small samples
    • Interviewer may influence responses

    Example: semi-structured interviews with five early years practitioners about outdoor learning.

    Focus groups

    A focus group is a guided discussion with a small group, usually 4 to 8 people.

    Strengths
    • Participants build on each other's ideas
    • Gathers several views at once
    Limitations
    • Dominant voices can take over
    • Confidentiality cannot be guaranteed among members
    • Group pressure may affect answers

    Example: a focus group of sixth form students discussing revision strategies.

    Observations

    An observation involves watching and recording behaviour. It may be structured (using a checklist) or unstructured (detailed field notes), participant (the researcher joins in) or non-participant, and overt (participants know) or covert (they do not; this is rarely ethically acceptable).

    Strengths
    • Records what people do, not just what they say
    • Captures behaviour in natural settings
    Limitations
    • The observer effect may change behaviour
    • Observer interpretation may be biased
    • Does not reveal reasons for behaviour

    Example: a structured observation of how often pupils ask questions during group work.

    Experiments

    An experiment changes the IV and measures the effect on the DV under controlled conditions. In education, most experiments are field experiments or quasi-experiments carried out in real classrooms.

    Strengths
    • Can establish cause and effect
    • Can be repeated to check findings
    Limitations
    • Hard to control all variables in real settings
    • Ethical concerns about withholding a potentially helpful intervention

    Example: comparing test scores of two classes, one taught with worked examples and one without.

    Document analysis

    Document analysis examines existing documents, such as policies, inspection reports, lesson plans, minutes or pupils' work.

    Strengths
    • Data already exist
    • Unaffected by the researcher's presence
    • Useful for tracking change over time
    Limitations
    • Documents may be incomplete or written for another purpose
    • Access may be restricted

    Example: analysing the behaviour policies of ten secondary schools to compare approaches to rewards.

    Key points
    • Probability sampling uses random selection and supports generalisation; non-probability sampling is practical but limits generalisation.
    • Choose sampling and data collection methods that fit your research question.
    • Every method has strengths and limitations that you should acknowledge in your project.

    6. Reliability, Validity and Bias

    Reliability, validity and bias are the main criteria used to judge the quality of a research design. You will need to evaluate your own project using these ideas.

    Reliability

    Reliability refers to consistency. If the study or measure were repeated under the same conditions, would it produce similar results?

    • Test-retest reliability: the same test gives similar results with the same people on different occasions.
    • Inter-rater reliability: different observers or markers produce the same results.
    • Internal consistency: items intended to measure the same thing produce similar responses.

    Improving reliability: use standardised instructions, clear coding schemes and checklists, train observers and pilot your instruments.

    Validity

    Validity refers to accuracy. Does the research measure what it claims to measure, and are the conclusions justified?

    Internal validity

    Internal validity is confidence that the IV, and not some other factor, caused the change in the DV.

    Threats: confounding variables, pupils maturing over time, outside events, practice effects from repeated testing and attrition.

    External validity

    External validity is the extent to which findings apply beyond the study to other people, settings and times.

    It includes ecological validity: whether findings reflect real-life settings.

    Construct validity asks whether a measure captures the concept it claims to. For example, a handwriting speed test is not a valid measure of reading comprehension.

    How reliability and validity relate

    A measure can be reliable without being valid, but it cannot be valid unless it is reliable. The target diagrams illustrate this: the centre represents the true value.

    Quality in qualitative research: trustworthiness

    Qualitative researchers often judge quality through trustworthiness (Lincoln and Guba, 1985), which has four parts:

    • Credibility: are the findings a believable account of participants' views? (For example, checking interpretations with participants.)
    • Transferability: is there enough detail about the context for readers to judge whether findings apply elsewhere?
    • Dependability: is the research process clearly documented, for example through an audit trail?
    • Confirmability: are findings based on the data rather than the researcher's preferences? Reflexivity supports this.

    Common sources of bias

    Bias is any systematic error that distorts findings.

    Types of bias and how to reduce them
    TypeWhat it isExampleHow to reduce it
    Sampling biasSome groups are more likely to be selected or to respond than othersOnly parents who attend parents' evening complete the questionnaireUse probability sampling where possible; follow up non-responders; report the limitation
    Researcher biasThe researcher's expectations influence design, data collection or interpretation, including confirmation biasAsking "How much has the intervention helped you?" assumes it has helpedUse neutral questions; follow standard procedures; practise reflexivity; ask someone else to check coding
    Response biasParticipants give inaccurate answers, including social desirability biasPupils over-report time spent reading because they think it pleases the teacherGuarantee anonymity; use neutral wording; avoid interviewing your own pupils where possible

    Triangulation

    Triangulation means using more than one source, method, researcher or theory to cross-check findings. If different sources point to the same conclusion, confidence in the findings increases.

    • Data triangulation: different people, times or places
    • Method triangulation: for example questionnaires, interviews and observation
    • Investigator triangulation: more than one researcher analysing the data
    • Theory triangulation: interpreting data through more than one theoretical lens
    Example

    To evaluate a new reading scheme, a researcher compares reading test data, interviews with teachers and observations of reading lessons. Agreement across all three strengthens the conclusions.

    Checklist for evaluating a research design
    • Is the research question clear and appropriate for the chosen design?
    • Is the sample suitable, and how far can findings be generalised?
    • Were instruments piloted and procedures standardised (reliability)?
    • Do the measures capture what they claim to (validity)?
    • Have extraneous and confounding variables been controlled or acknowledged?
    • What sources of bias may be present, and how were they reduced?
    • Was triangulation used?
    • Were ethical principles followed throughout?
    Key points
    • Reliability is consistency; validity is accuracy. Valid research must be reliable.
    • Internal validity concerns cause and effect; external validity concerns generalisability.
    • Sampling, researcher and response bias can all distort findings. Triangulation strengthens conclusions.
